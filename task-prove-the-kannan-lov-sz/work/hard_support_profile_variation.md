# Hard support, free-boundary profile curvature, and flat-sheet intersection

## 0. Outcome

This note closes the **exact regular** hard-support branch and isolates the
quantitative obstruction.

Let `Omega` be a smooth convex body, let

\[
                         d\mu=Z^{-1}e^{-V}1_\Omega dx,              \tag{0.1}
\]

where `V` is smooth and convex up to the boundary, and let `E` be a regular
isoperimetric region.  Its interior interface `Sigma` meets `partial Omega`
orthogonally.  For a normal speed `u`, the exact constrained second variation
is

\[
 \boxed{\begin{aligned}
 Q_\Sigma(u)={}&\int_\Sigma
   \left(|\nabla_\Sigma u|^2-
   (|\mathrm {II}_\Sigma|^2+\nabla^2V(N,N))u^2\right)d\sigma_\mu\\
 &-\int_{\partial\Sigma}
   \mathrm {II}_{\partial\Omega}(N,N)u^2d\tau_\mu.
 \end{aligned}}                                                \tag{0.2}
\]

Here the outward-normal convention makes
`II_(partial Omega)>=0`.  Thus the support term has the same favorable sign
as ambient log-convexity.  Testing with `u=1` gives the profile support
inequality

\[
 \mathcal I''(v)\le-{\mathcal E(\Sigma_v)\over\mathcal I(v)^2},
 \quad
 \mathcal E(\Sigma):=
 \int_\Sigma(|\mathrm {II}|^2+\nabla^2V(N,N))d\sigma_\mu
 +\int_{\partial\Sigma}\mathrm {II}_{\partial\Omega}(N,N)d\tau_\mu.
                                                               \tag{0.3}
\]

At every volume where the classical second derivative and a regular
minimizer exist, (0.3) is literal.  An integrated version using the curvature
measure of the concave profile is proved below.

If `mathcal I(v)/v` is exactly constant on an interval, every regular leaf
in that interval has

\[
 \mathrm {II}_\Sigma=0,\qquad \nabla^2V(N,N)=0,
 \qquad \mathrm {II}_{\partial\Omega}(N,N)=0.             \tag{0.4}
\]

The apparent hard-support escape--several nonparallel flat sheets ending on
different support faces--does not occur in the regular equality case.  Two
nonparallel planar free-boundary sections of one convex body must intersect
in its interior.  The proof projects the body onto the two-plane spanned by
their normals; both projected sections are affine diameters of the planar
projection, and affine diameters intersect.  Hence disjoint regular boundary
components are parallel.  The set is then the inverse image of a
one-dimensional finite-perimeter set under one affine marginal.  Isotropy
and the one-dimensional log-concave Cheeger bound give a universal lower
bound for its perimeter ratio.

Near equality gives a sharp but weaker statement.  If

\[
 {\mathcal I(a)\over a}\le(1+\delta){\mathcal I(b)\over b},
 \qquad 0<a<b\le1/2,                                      \tag{0.5}
\]

then, for `0<s<(b-a)/2`,

\[
 \boxed{
 \int_{a+s}^{b-s}{\mathcal E(\Sigma_v)\over\mathcal I(v)^2}dv
 \le {2\delta\mathcal I(b)\over s},
 \qquad
 \int_{a+s}^{b-s}\mathcal E(\Sigma_v)dv
 \le {2\delta\mathcal I(b)^3\over s}.}                  \tag{0.6}
\]

This is only an `L^1`-in-volume, `L^2`-in-curvature estimate.  It supplies
neither a uniform reach nor a ridge/contact conductance.  In dimensions
relevant to rank `17`, a fixed normal rotation can be hidden in a neck whose
`int |II|^2` tends to zero.  Moreover, (0.6) makes the local negative second
variation of order `p^3`, while the required physical saving is
`10^-4 p`; a displacement of order `1/p`, hence global geometry, is needed.

The numerical physical data

\[
 \operatorname {tr}M_{\rm phys}>.004p,qquad
 \operatorname {rank}_{\rm eff}M_{\rm phys}>17,qquad
 \mathfrak G_{\rm fin}\le6.02\,10^{-5}p                  \tag{0.7}
\]

do not provide that reach or conductance.  Exact equality is therefore
classified, but the desired fixed finite splice does not follow from profile
near-linearity and second variation alone.

## 1. First variation and the contact angle

Assume throughout Sections 1--3 that `Omega subset R^n` is bounded, convex,
and `C^3`, that `rho=Z^{-1}e^{-V}` is positive and `C^2` on
`closure Omega`, and that `V` is convex.  Let `E` have a compact `C^3`
interior boundary

\[
 \Sigma=\overline{\partial E\cap\Omega},\qquad
 \Gamma=\partial\Sigma\subset\partial\Omega.             \tag{1.1}
\]

Let `N` be the outer normal of `E` along `Sigma`, let `eta` be the outward
conormal of `Gamma` in `Sigma`, and let `nu` be the outward normal of
`Omega`.  Variations are generated by vector fields `X` tangent to
`partial Omega`; put `u=X dot N`.

With

\[
 H_\mu=\operatorname {tr}\mathrm {II}_\Sigma
                         -\langle\nabla V,N\rangle,         \tag{1.2}
\]

the first variations are

\[
 \delta\mu(E)=\int_\Sigma u\,d\sigma_\mu,                 \tag{1.3}
\]

\[
 \delta P_\mu(E)=\int_\Sigma H_\mu u\,d\sigma_\mu
                  +\int_\Gamma\langle\eta,X\rangle d\tau_\mu. \tag{1.4}
\]

For a volume-constrained minimizer, arbitrary interior speeds give
`H_mu=lambda`.  Arbitrary boundary vector fields tangent to
`partial Omega` then give

\[
 P_{T\partial\Omega}\eta=0,qquad \eta=\nu,qquad
                              \langle N,\nu\rangle=0.       \tag{1.5}
\]

Thus the contact angle is exactly `pi/2`.

For later use, if a nonstationary comparison surface has angle defect, put

\[
 \zeta=P_{T\partial\Omega}\eta,qquad
                         |\zeta|=|\langle N,\nu\rangle|.   \tag{1.6}
\]

Choosing on a contact patch `X=-chi zeta`, followed by a separate volume
correction, makes the boundary part of (1.4)

\[
                         -\int_\Gamma\chi|\zeta|^2d\tau_\mu. \tag{1.7}
\]

If the interior weighted mean curvature is one common constant on the active
patch and compensator, then for one fixed `C^2` choice this yields, with all
orientations fixed as above,

\[
 P_\mu(E_t)-P_\mu(E)
 =-t\int_\Gamma\chi|\zeta|^2d\tau_\mu+O_{E,\Omega,V,\chi}(t^2),
 \qquad \mu(E_t)=\mu(E).                                  \tag{1.8}
\]

Hence a genuine contact-angle defect is a first-order splice source.  Turning
(1.7) into a fixed finite saving still requires a patch of controlled size,
a controlled compensator, and a trace-preserving cutoff.  The scalar
near-linearity of the isoperimetric profile supplies none of these.  For the
exact isoperimetric surfaces considered below, `zeta=0` identically.

## 2. Exact free-boundary second variation

Use the convention

\[
 \mathrm {II}_{\partial\Omega}(Y,Y)
       =\langle D_Y\nu,Y\rangle\ge0                       \tag{2.1}
\]

on a convex support.  The following formula includes the acceleration needed
to keep the flow on `partial Omega`.

**Lemma 2.1 (weighted free-boundary index form).**  If (1.5) holds and
`H_mu=lambda`, every smooth normal speed `u` is realized by an admissible
flow and

\[
 {d^2\over dt^2}\bigg|_{0}
       \{P_\mu(E_t)-\lambda\mu(E_t)\}=Q_\Sigma(u),          \tag{2.2}
\]

where `Q_Sigma` is (0.2).

**Proof.**  In the interior, differentiating weighted mean curvature gives

\[
 (H_\mu)'=-\Delta_\Sigma u
 +\langle\nabla_\Sigma V,\nabla_\Sigma u\rangle
 -( |\mathrm {II}_\Sigma|^2+\nabla^2V(N,N))u.              \tag{2.3}
\]

Weighted integration by parts produces the bulk integral in (0.2) and the
conormal term

\[
                         -\int_\Gamma u\,\partial_\eta u\,d\tau_\mu. \tag{2.3a}
\]

Along `Gamma`, `N` is tangent to `partial Omega`.  If `gamma(t)` is a
contact-point trajectory with `gamma'(0)=uN`, differentiating
`langle nu(gamma(t)),gamma'(t)rangle=0` gives

\[
 \langle\nu,\gamma''(0)\rangle
       =-\mathrm {II}_{\partial\Omega}(uN,uN).             \tag{2.3b}
\]

The derivative of the conormal term in the first-variation formula is

\[
 \int_\Gamma u\,\partial_\eta u\,d\tau_\mu
 -\int_\Gamma\mathrm {II}_{\partial\Omega}(N,N)u^2d\tau_\mu. \tag{2.3c}
\]

The first term in (2.3c) cancels (2.3a), while (2.3b) supplies its second
term.  The result is

\[
              -\int_\Gamma\mathrm {II}_{\partial\Omega}(N,N)
                         u^2d\tau_\mu.                      \tag{2.4}
\]

The density derivatives combine with (2.3) into `nabla^2V(N,N)`; there is
no additional `partial_nu V` boundary term.  This proves (0.2).  QED.

In particular, for `u=1`,

\[
                         Q_\Sigma(1)=-\mathcal E(\Sigma).  \tag{2.5}
\]

All three terms defining `mathcal E` are nonnegative.

### 2.1 Profile support inequality

Let `v=mu(E)`, `P=mathcal I(v)=P_mu(E)`, and use an admissible variation with
initial speed `u=1`.  Its volume derivative is `v'(0)=P`.  If `P(t)` is the
competitor perimeter, then

\[
 \mathcal I(v(t))\le P(t),\qquad
 \mathcal I(v(0))=P(0),\qquad \mathcal I'(v)=\lambda.      \tag{2.6}
\]

Twice differentiating at a classical twice-differentiability point gives

\[
 \mathcal I''(v)P^2
 \le P''(0)-\lambda v''(0)=Q_\Sigma(1)
 =-\mathcal E(\Sigma),                                    \tag{2.7}
\]

which is (0.3).  No nested family of minimizers is needed for this
pointwise support inequality.

If `mathcal I` is affine near `v`, (2.7) and convexity imply (0.4).  Since a
positive-semidefinite matrix `A` satisfies
`x^TAx=0 implies Ax=0`, equality also gives

\[
 \nabla^2V\,N=0\quad\hbox{on }\Sigma,qquad
 S_{\partial\Omega}N=0\quad\hbox{on }\Gamma.              \tag{2.8}
\]

### 2.2 Quantitative near-linearity

The next statement uses only concavity after (2.7).

**Lemma 2.2 (profile curvature budget).**  Let `0<a<b<=1/2`, put

\[
 c={\mathcal I(b)\over b},\qquad p_b=\mathcal I(b),
 \qquad L=b-a,                                             \tag{2.9}
\]

and suppose

\[
                      q(a)\le(1+\delta)q(b).              \tag{2.10}
\]

Assume that for almost every `v in (a,b)` the profile is classically twice
differentiable and has a regular minimizer `E_v`.  Then, for
`0<s<L/2`, (0.6) holds.  In particular,

\[
 \int_{a+L/4}^{b-L/4}\mathcal E(\Sigma_v)dv
 \le {8\delta p_b^3\over L},                              \tag{2.11}
\]

and some volume in the middle half satisfies

\[
                         \mathcal E(\Sigma_v)
 \le {16\delta p_b^3\over L^2}.                           \tag{2.12}
\]

Since the three summands of `mathcal E` are nonnegative, (0.6) and (2.11)
hold separately with `mathcal E` replaced by any one of

\[
 \int_{\Sigma_v}|\mathrm {II}_{\Sigma_v}|^2d\sigma_\mu,
 \qquad
 \int_{\Sigma_v}\nabla^2V(N_v,N_v)d\sigma_\mu,
 \qquad
 \int_{\partial\Sigma_v}\mathrm {II}_{\partial\Omega}(N_v,N_v)d\tau_\mu.
                                                               \tag{2.12a}
\]

**Proof.**  Set `J(t)=mathcal I(t)-ct`.  Concavity and monotonicity of `q`
give, throughout `[a,b]`,

\[
                         0\le J(t)\le\delta ct\le\delta p_b. \tag{2.13}
\]

Let `kappa=-D^2mathcal I=-D^2J` be the nonnegative curvature measure of the
concave profile.  Concavity gives

\[
 \begin{aligned}
 J'_-(a+s)&\le {J(a+s)-J(a)\over s}\le{\delta p_b\over s},\\
 J'_+(b-s)&\ge {J(b)-J(b-s)\over s}\ge-{\delta p_b\over s}.
 \end{aligned}                                            \tag{2.14}
\]

Therefore

\[
 \kappa([a+s,b-s])
 =J'_-(a+s)-J'_+(b-s)\le{2\delta p_b\over s}.             \tag{2.15}
\]

At almost every twice-differentiability point, (2.7) says

\[
 {\mathcal E(\Sigma_v)\over\mathcal I(v)^2}
 \le-\mathcal I''(v).                                     \tag{2.16}
\]

Integrating (2.16) is bounded by the full measure in (2.15), proving the
first inequality in (0.6).  The profile is nondecreasing on `(0,1/2]`, so
`mathcal I(v)<=p_b`; this proves the second inequality.  Taking `s=L/4`
and then averaging over an interval of length `L/2` proves (2.11)--(2.12).
QED.

This is the strongest conclusion obtainable from the scalar hypothesis
(2.10) without additional spatial stability.  Curvature may concentrate on
a null set of volumes or on a small subset of one hypersurface.

## 3. Flat free-boundary sections of a convex body

The exact hard-support geometry admits a clean reduction to dimension two.

**Lemma 3.1 (two planar free-boundary sections intersect).**  Let `Omega` be
a full-dimensional compact convex body with `C^1` boundary.  For `i=1,2`,
let

\[
 H_i=\{x:\langle N_i,x\rangle=c_i\},\qquad
 \Sigma_i=H_i\cap\Omega,                                  \tag{3.1}
\]

where `N_1,N_2` are nonparallel unit vectors and `Sigma_i` has nonempty
relative interior in `H_i`.  Suppose each section is free-boundary:

\[
 \langle N_i,\nu_\Omega(x)\rangle=0
       \quad\hbox{for every }x\in H_i\cap\partial\Omega.  \tag{3.2}
\]

Then

\[
                         H_1\cap H_2\cap\operatorname {int}\Omega
 \ne\varnothing.                                          \tag{3.3}
\]

**Proof.**  Let `P=span{N_1,N_2}` and let `pi` be orthogonal projection onto
`P`.  Put `D=pi(Omega)`, a planar convex body, and

\[
                         ell_i=\{z\in P:\langle N_i,z\rangle=c_i\}. \tag{3.4}
\]

Because `N_i in P`,

\[
                         \pi(\Sigma_i)=D\cap\ell_i.       \tag{3.5}
\]

Let `t_i` be a unit direction vector along `ell_i`.  At either endpoint `z`
of the chord `D cap ell_i`, choose a supporting vector `w in P` of `D` and
a lift `x in Omega` with `pi(x)=z`.  The same `w` supports `Omega` at `x`.
Smoothness gives `nu_Omega(x)` parallel to `w`.  Since `x in H_i`, (3.2)
gives `w dot N_i=0`; hence `w` is parallel to `t_i`.  The two endpoints of
`D cap ell_i` therefore lie on opposite parallel support lines with normals
`plus/minus t_i`.  In planar convexity terminology, `D cap ell_i` is an
affine diameter.

The endpoints of two affine diameters with nonparallel directions alternate
on the boundary of a planar convex body: their outer support normals occur
cyclically as `t_1,t_2,-t_1,-t_2`, up to reversing orientation.  The two
chords consequently intersect in their relative interiors.  Let
`z_0 in int(D)` be an intersection point.  Linear projections map the
interior of a full-dimensional convex body onto the interior of its
projection, so there is `x_0 in int(Omega)` with `pi(x_0)=z_0`.  Equations
(3.4) give `x_0 in H_1 cap H_2`, proving (3.3).  QED.

**Lemma 3.2 (a connected flat sheet is a full section).**  Let `Sigma` be a
connected embedded flat hypersurface in `Omega`, with no boundary in
`int(Omega)` and with `partial Sigma subset partial Omega`.  Then for its
containing hyperplane `H`,

\[
                              \Sigma=H\cap\Omega.          \tag{3.6}
\]

**Proof.**  The set `H cap int(Omega)` is convex and connected.  The relative
interior of `Sigma` is both open and closed in it: a failure of closedness
would create an interior boundary point.  It is nonempty, hence equals the
whole section.  Taking closures proves (3.6).  QED.

Together, the lemmas show that pairwise disjoint flat regular free-boundary
components must have one common projective normal.

### 3.1 Exact affine-marginal closure

**Proposition 3.3 (exact regular hard-support rigidity).**  Let `mu` be as in
Section 1 and isotropic.  Let `E` be a regular isoperimetric region of volume
`v<=1/2`.  Suppose `mathcal I` is affine on a neighborhood of `v`, and that
`partial E cap Omega` has finitely many connected components.  Then all
components are parallel full hyperplane sections.  There are a unit vector
`N` and a one-dimensional finite-perimeter set `J` such that

\[
                         E=\{x\in\Omega:\langle N,x\rangle\in J\}
 \quad\hbox{up to }\mu\hbox{-null sets}.                    \tag{3.7}
\]

Consequently, if `P_mu(E)=c mu(E)`, then

\[
                              c\ge c_{1d}>0,               \tag{3.8}
\]

where `c_(1d)` is a universal one-dimensional isotropic log-concave Cheeger
constant.

**Proof.**  Equations (2.7) and (0.4) make every component flat.  Lemma 3.2
makes it a full hyperplane section.  Distinct boundary components cannot
intersect, so Lemma 3.1 makes all their normals parallel.  The parallel
sections divide `Omega` into slabs.  Each slice of a slab by a hyperplane
orthogonal to `N` is convex and connected and contains no boundary of `E`;
membership is therefore constant on that slice.  This proves (3.7).

The marginal `nu=N_#mu` is log-concave and has variance one.  Weighted
coarea for the finitely many endpoints of `J` gives
`P_mu(E)=P_nu(J)`.  Hence

\[
 {P_\mu(E)\over\mu(E)}={P_\nu(J)\over\nu(J)}
 \ge\psi_\nu\ge c_{1d},                                  \tag{3.9}
\]

which proves (3.8).  QED.

This proposition removes the hard-support gap in the **exact**, regular,
finite-component version of Proposition 3.2 in
`profile_linearity_separation.md`.  It does not assume a nested normal
foliation.

## 4. Why the quantitative conclusion does not follow

### 4.1 Constant transfers between components

Suppose two disjoint stationary components have common multiplier `lambda`.
Write `P_i=sigma_mu(Sigma_i)` and `E_i=mathcal E(Sigma_i)`.  Give them
constant speeds `a_1,a_2` satisfying

\[
                              a_1P_1+a_2P_2=0.             \tag{4.1}
\]

After the usual second-order scalar correction, the volume is exactly fixed
and

\[
 P_\mu(E_t)=P_\mu(E)
 -{t^2\over2}(a_1^2E_1+a_2^2E_2)+o(t^2).                 \tag{4.2}
\]

Thus positive bulk or contact energy is indeed a same-mass splice source.
But a fixed saving requires a uniform displacement range and a uniform
bound on the remainder.  Smoothness, convexity, and isotropy give no uniform
`C^3` bound or reach for either the support or the minimizing surface.

More importantly, near-linearity makes the coefficient small.  On a fixed
volume interval, (2.12) is of order `delta p_b^3`.  A unit-size deformation
therefore saves only order `delta p_b^3`; to obtain `10^-4 p_b` from (4.2)
one needs

\[
                         |t|\gtrsim {10^{-2}\over\sqrt\delta\,p_b}. \tag{4.3}
\]

For the large-`K` regime, `p_b` is very small.  Equation (4.3) is a global
tail-scale displacement, not a local contact variation.

### 4.2 Small curvature does not give uniform normal alignment

The energy in (0.6) is quadratic.  If the hypersurface dimension is
`d=n-1>=4`, two large almost-planar pieces with a fixed angle can be connected
through a tube of radius `epsilon`.  A tube of bounded length has area
`O(epsilon^(d-1))` and curvature `O(epsilon^-1)`, so

\[
                              \int|\mathrm {II}|^2
 =O(\varepsilon^{d-3})\longrightarrow0.                    \tag{4.4}
\]

The normal still rotates by a fixed angle.  Rank `17` operates in dimensions
where this capacity collapse is available.  This construction is not claimed
to be isoperimetric; it proves the precise logical point that (0.6) cannot be
converted into uniform flat-sheet geometry without a new quantitative
regularity or capacity theorem.

The same issue appears at the support.  An isotropic-up-to-scale smooth
example is the superellipsoid

\[
                         \Omega_m=\{x:\sum_i|x_i|^{2m}<1\},
 \qquad m\ge2.                                            \tag{4.5}
\]

For the coordinate section `Sigma_i=Omega_m cap {x_i=0}`,

\[
 \mathrm {II}_{\Sigma_i}=0,qquad
 \langle e_i,\nu_{\Omega_m}\rangle=0,qquad
 \mathrm {II}_{\partial\Omega_m}(e_i,e_i)=0              \tag{4.6}
\]

along its contact boundary.  All coordinate directions therefore have zero
index-form energy, although their normals have full effective rank.  The
sections intersect, in agreement with Lemma 3.1.  Adding an arbitrarily small
quadratic term to the defining function makes the support strictly convex
while making the contact energy arbitrarily small.  Thus smoothness and
isotropy have no uniform contact-curvature gap.

### 4.3 The numerical rank has only Boolean intersection content here

In the same-level branch of `two_patch_physical_splicing.md`, the rank
`R>17` gives angular energy at least `8/17`.  If one had a bounded-overlap
intersection conductance capturing half that energy and converted it with
the `1/8` bevel coefficient, then

\[
 {(.004p)(8/17)\over16}=1.176\,10^{-4}p>10^{-4}p.          \tag{4.7}
\]

Lemma 3.1 supplies only the statement that two exact nonparallel flat full
sections intersect.  It gives no lower bound on the weighted
`(n-2)`-dimensional size of the intersection and no bounded-reuse matching of
many pairs.  A tiny neck or focal set can carry many Boolean intersections.
The approximately `17.6%` numerical slack in (4.7) is therefore irrelevant:
the missing conductance can tend to zero.

There is also no direct conversion of `6.02*10^-5 p` into the `delta` of
(2.10).  Component additivity gives `delta<=epsilon/eta` only when a
component has **volume** at least `eta` times the total.  The physical number
`.004p` is boundary flux, not volume, and yields no positive `eta`.  Even the
optimistic substitution `eta=.004` would give

\[
                         \delta\le {6.02\,10^{-5}\over.004}=0.01505, \tag{4.8}
\]

which still leaves (0.6) as an integrated quadratic estimate rather than a
finite splice.

## 5. Singular sets and nonsmooth supports

The preceding exact argument assumes regularity up to `Gamma`.  For a
standard codimension-at-least-seven singular set, the constant speed can be
approximated by cutoffs `chi_k` with

\[
 0\le\chi_k\le1,qquad \chi_k\to1,qquad
                         \int_\Sigma|\nabla\chi_k|^2d\sigma_\mu\to0. \tag{5.1}
\]

If the three nonnegative curvature/contact terms are integrable, monotone
or lower-semicontinuous passage in (0.2) gives (0.3).  If one is not
integrable, its value is `+infinity`, which is incompatible with profile
near-linearity at a classical twice-differentiability point.  A complete
application nevertheless needs the corresponding free-boundary singular-set
capacity theorem and control of boundary singularities; these are not proved
here.

In the exact flat case, two nonparallel sheets intersect in an interior
codimension-two set.  Such an intersection cannot be hidden in an
isoperimetric singular set of codimension at least seven.  It must instead be
an actual ridge/focal junction, where the finite bevel mechanism applies if
there is quantitative weighted capacity.

For a polytope, `II_(partial Omega)=0` on open facets and is not a classical
function on edges.  Smooth approximation moves the missing contact charge
onto the rounded edge layers.  Passing to the limit requires retaining the
resulting positive boundary-curvature measure.  Dropping it would incorrectly
treat a nonorthogonal simplex contact as a free flat equality case.  The cube
coordinate cut is different: its normal lies in the null direction of every
contact facet, and the rounded-edge contribution tends to zero.

## 6. Model audit

### 6.1 Cube

A coordinate cut of a cube is a full flat section.  It meets every contacted
side facet orthogonally and

\[
 |\mathrm {II}_\Sigma|^2=\nabla^2V(N,N)
 =\mathrm {II}_{\partial\Omega}(N,N)=0.                    \tag{6.1}
\]

It is the affine marginal branch.  Smooth superellipsoid approximations
(4.5) retain the exact null contact direction.  Different coordinate
sections intersect, so a single smooth boundary cannot contain them as
disjoint components.  An inner box instead has interior ridges, handled by
the bevel.

The uniform coordinate marginal has a constant, rather than through-origin
linear, slab perimeter.  Thus `q(v)=I(v)/v` is not constant on a fixed
relative volume interval; the exponential equality branch is not falsely
triggered.

### 6.2 Simplex

A generic barycentric hyperplane meets some simplex facets at a nonright
angle.  It is therefore not a regular free-boundary stationary surface for
relative perimeter; (1.7) detects the contact-angle defect.  On smooth
approximations, the same information is carried by the contact and rounded
edge terms.  A genuinely free flat section is subject to Lemma 3.1.  Inner
homothetic simplices have ordinary dihedral ridges and are bevelled.

### 6.3 Cylinder and exponential cylinder

For `Omega=D times I`, a cross-section normal to the axis is flat, meets the
lateral support orthogonally, and lies in the null direction of its second
fundamental form.  If

\[
                         V(z,t)=W(z)+\kappa t,             \tag{6.2}
\]

then `nabla^2V(N,N)=0` as well.  Parallel cross-sections are exactly the
product/affine marginal branch.  Whenever their marginal profile is linear
through the origin, the active one-dimensional tail is exponential and the
variance-one marginal gives the universal bound (3.8).  A finite truncated
cylinder can instead have an affine profile with a nonzero intercept; this
does not make `I(v)/v` constant.

### 6.4 Gaussian and the Euclidean ball

For a Gaussian radial sphere in full space there is no support contact, and

\[
 \mathcal E=\int_\Sigma\left({n-1\over R^2}+1\right)d\sigma_\gamma>0. \tag{6.3}
\]

It cannot lie in a linear-profile equality branch.  Gaussian halfspaces are
the flat affine branch.

For uniform measure on a hard Euclidean ball, the equatorial disk has zero
bulk curvature but

\[
 \int_{\partial\Sigma}\mathrm {II}_{\partial\Omega}(N,N)d\tau
 ={1\over R}\mathcal H^{n-2}(\partial\Sigma)>0.            \tag{6.4}
\]

This is exactly the strict concavity of the relative profile under parallel
displacement.  Different equatorial disks intersect at the center.

### 6.5 The density `exp(-||x||_infty)`

This full-support log-concave density has no support-boundary term.  Its
potential is affine inside each max cell and its Hessian is a positive
measure on the cell walls.  Cube-level facet cores consequently have zero
classical energy, but nonparallel facets meet at codimension-two ridges.
Smooth approximation must retain the wall/focal charge.  This is the density
analogue of the hard-support warning in Section 5, not a separated
free-boundary equality case.

## 7. Formal remaining quantitative lemma

The exact result suggests the following sufficient statement, which is not
proved by second variation:

> If regular or quantitatively regular free-boundary sheets carry physical
> flux `a`, their projective normal law has same-level angular energy `W`,
> and their profile-curvature budget is as in (0.6), then either they are
> within controlled error of parallel full sections, or their interior/contact
> junctions admit a bounded-reuse conductance at least `aW/2`.

Together with (4.7), this statement would close the hard-support same-level
branch.  Its conductance conclusion is substantially stronger than (0.6):
the neck scaling (4.4) and the superellipsoid null directions show that it
cannot be obtained from integrated `|II|^2`, support curvature, isotropy, and
normal rank alone.  It requires quantitative isoperimetric regularity or a
new convex Frankel-to-capacity theorem.

Thus the present audit yields a complete exact regular classification and an
explicit curvature budget with constants, but not the fixed
`10^-4 p` finite splice demanded by the near-extremal physical construction.
