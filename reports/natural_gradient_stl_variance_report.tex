\begin{abstract}
Stochastic Gaussian natural-gradient variational inference estimates the Gaussian
expectations that drive the Fisher--Rao flow from samples
$\theta\sim\N(m,C)$. The \emph{Sticking-the-Landing} (STL) trick replaces the
plain posterior-score mean estimator $\bbase(\theta)=\score(\theta)$ by the
score-\emph{residual} estimator
$\bstl(\theta)=\score(\theta)+C^{-1}(\theta-m)$, which subtracts the
(zero-mean) score of the variational Gaussian itself. The residual estimator is
unbiased and, at a well-specified Gaussian optimum, vanishes \emph{pointwise}. We
isolate the effect of mean-block STL -- holding the covariance update fixed --
with two experiments (an estimator-level variance comparison at fixed states and
an algorithm-level trajectory comparison) on a well-specified anisotropic
Gaussian target and a misspecified smooth strongly log-concave $\log\cosh$
target, swept over dimension, conditioning, and (for $\log\cosh$) the
non-Gaussianity strength $\tau$.

The findings are clean. For the Gaussian target the STL mean estimator has
\emph{exactly zero} variance whenever $C=C_\star$ (the optimum and every
mean-only perturbation); because the Hessian sampler is moreover deterministic
(the Gaussian curvature is state-independent), STL collapses the algorithm's
stochastic noise floor all the way to machine precision, while the baseline
retains a floor set by its mean noise. For the misspecified $\log\cosh$ target
STL still reduces the floor by roughly three orders of magnitude but does
\emph{not} remove it, because the nonlinear part of the score is not captured by
the Gaussian residual. STL is not universally beneficial: when the
covariance is \emph{under-dispersed} ($C\prec C_\star$, so $C^{-1}$ is large) the
residual term inflates the variance above the baseline. For the direct Hessian
sampler used here the covariance-block ``STL'' rewrite is algebraically identical
to the original covariance update, so the experiment genuinely isolates the mean
block. We conclude that mean-STL is an essentially cost-free, practically
effective variance-reduction device for Gaussian variational families -- best
near the optimum and under correct Gaussian specification -- but not a
theorem-level substitute for a stochastic-variance assumption.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

We approximate a target posterior $\rho_\post\propto e^{-V}$ on $\R^d$ by a
Gaussian $q_a=\N(m,C)$, $C\in\SPD(d)$, with state $a=(m,C)$, minimizing the
reverse KL $\mathcal{E}(a)=\E_{q_a}[V]-\tfrac12\log\det C$ (up to an
$a$-independent constant). The Gaussian \emph{natural-gradient} (Fisher--Rao)
flow is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:flow}
\dot m = C\,\E_{q_a}[\score(\theta)],
\qquad
\dot C = C + C\,\E_{q_a}[\nabla^2\log\rho_\post(\theta)]\,C,
\end{equation}
with $\score(\theta)=\grad\log\rho_\post(\theta)=-\grad V(\theta)$ and
$\nabla^2\log\rho_\post=-\grad^2V$. A stochastic algorithm draws
$\theta_n\sim\N(m_n,C_n)$ and replaces the two expectations in
\eqref{eq:flow} by sample estimators. The one-sample baselines are
$b_n=\score(\theta_n)$ and $S_n=\nabla^2\log\rho_\post(\theta_n)$, and the mean
update is $m_{n+1}=m_n+\Delta t\,C_n b_n$.

The estimator $\score(\theta)$ is noisy even at the optimum: for a Gaussian
target the score is linear, $\score(\theta)=-A\theta$, so $b_n$ fluctuates with
$\theta_n$ while its \emph{mean} is what the flow needs. Sticking the Landing
\cite{stl} removes the part of this fluctuation that the variational family can
account for analytically.

\section{Theory: what STL changes, and what it does not}
\label{sec:theory}

\paragraph{Mean block.}
For a Gaussian $q_a=\N(m,C)$ the variational score is explicit,
\begin{equation}
\grad_\theta\log q_a(\theta) = -\,C^{-1}(\theta-m).
\end{equation}
The STL mean estimator subtracts it from the posterior score,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bstl}
\bstl(\theta)
 = \score(\theta) - \grad_\theta\log q_a(\theta)
 = \score(\theta) + C^{-1}(\theta-m).
\end{equation}
Because $\E_{q_a}[C^{-1}(\theta-m)]=0$, the estimator is \emph{unbiased} for
$\E_{q_a}[\score]$; this is the quantity the mean update needs, so swapping
$\bbase$ for $\bstl$ changes only the noise, not the target.

\begin{proposition}[Pointwise vanishing at a well-specified optimum]
\label{prop:zero}
If $\rho_\post=\N(m_\star,C_\star)$ is exactly Gaussian, then at $a=a_\star$ the
posterior score equals the negative variational score,
$\score(\theta)=-C_\star^{-1}(\theta-m_\star)$, and therefore
$\bstl(\theta)\equiv 0$ for every $\theta$.
\end{proposition}

So at a well-specified optimum the STL mean estimator has \emph{zero variance}.
More generally, for the Gaussian target $V=\tfrac12\theta^\top A\theta$ the STL
fluctuation is
$\bstl(\theta)-\E[\bstl] = (C^{-1}-A)(\theta-m)$,
which depends on $C$ but \emph{not} on $m$: it is exactly zero whenever
$C=C_\star=A^{-1}$, regardless of the mean. This is the precise sense in which
``correct Gaussian specification'' matters more than ``distance to the optimum.''

\paragraph{Covariance block (why it is not a separate estimator here).}
The natural residual rewrite of the curvature estimator is
$K(\theta)=\nabla^2\log\rho_\post(\theta)+C^{-1}=S(\theta)+C^{-1}$. For the
\emph{direct} Hessian sampler this is only an algebraic restatement of the
covariance update. With $S_n$ the sampled Hessian and $K_n=S_n+C_n^{-1}$,
\begin{align}
\text{Riemannian:}\quad
 & e^{\Delta t}\,C^{1/2}\exp\!\big(\Delta t\,C^{1/2}S_nC^{1/2}\big)C^{1/2}
 = C^{1/2}\exp\!\big(\Delta t\,C^{1/2}K_nC^{1/2}\big)C^{1/2},
 \label{eq:riem-equiv}\\
\text{KL:}\quad
 & (1+\Delta t)\big(C^{-1}-\Delta t\,S_n\big)^{-1}
 = (1+\Delta t)\big((1+\Delta t)C^{-1}-\Delta t\,K_n\big)^{-1},
 \label{eq:kl-equiv}
\end{align}
because $C^{1/2}K_nC^{1/2}=C^{1/2}S_nC^{1/2}+I$ and $I$ commutes with everything
(giving the $e^{\Delta t}$ factor in \eqref{eq:riem-equiv}), and because
$(1+\Delta t)C^{-1}-\Delta t K_n=C^{-1}-\Delta t S_n$ in \eqref{eq:kl-equiv}.
Both identities are verified numerically to machine precision in the test suite.
Consequently the covariance update is held \emph{fixed} across baseline and STL,
and the only algorithmic difference is the mean estimator $\bbase$ vs.\ $\bstl$.

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Targets.}
Both targets have a diagonal curvature $A=\mathrm{diag}(a)$ with $a$ log-spaced
in $[1,\kappa]$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Well-specified Gaussian:} $V(\theta)=\tfrac12\theta^\top A\theta$,
so $\score=-A\theta$, $\nabla^2\log\rho_\post=-A$, and the VI optimum is exact,
$m_\star=0$, $C_\star=A^{-1}$.
\item \textbf{Misspecified $\log\cosh$:}
$V(\theta)=\tfrac12\theta^\top A\theta+\tau\sum_i\log\cosh(\theta_i)$, so
$\score=-A\theta-\tau\tanh(\theta)$ and
$\nabla^2\log\rho_\post=-A-\tau\,\mathrm{diag}(\operatorname{sech}^2\theta_i)$.
The target is separable, strongly log-concave, and globally smooth but not
Gaussian. The optimum mean is $m_\star=0$ by symmetry; the diagonal $C_\star$ is
obtained by a deterministic per-coordinate fixed point
$c_i=1/(a_i+\tau\,\E_{\N(0,c_i)}[\operatorname{sech}^2])$ evaluated by
Gauss--Hermite quadrature (recorded stationarity residual $<10^{-9}$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Experiment A (estimator-level).}
At six fixed Gaussian states -- the optimum, three mean perturbations
(\emph{near}/\emph{medium}/\emph{far}, offsets $0.25/1/4$ marginal standard
deviations), and an \emph{under}- and \emph{over}-dispersed covariance
($\tfrac14C_\star$, $4C_\star$) -- we draw $M=10^5$ samples and compute, for both
estimators, the empirical bias against the deterministic reference
$\E_{q_a}[\score]$, the Euclidean trace variance $\VarE(b)=\E\|b-\E b\|^2$, the
Fisher--Rao mean-block variance $\VarFR(b)=\E[(b-\E b)^\top C(b-\E b)]$, and the
ratios $\VarFR(\bstl)/\VarFR(\bbase)$ and $\VarE(\bstl)/\VarE(\bbase)$. For the
Gaussian target the closed forms $\VarE(\bbase)=\Tr(ACA)$,
$\VarFR(\bbase)=\Tr(CACA)$, $\VarE(\bstl)=\Tr(DCD)$, $\VarFR(\bstl)=\Tr(CDCD)$
with $D=C^{-1}-A$ validate the Monte Carlo estimates.

\paragraph{Experiment B (algorithm-level).}
We run the four methods \texttt{riemannian}, \texttt{riemannian\_stl},
\texttt{kl}, and \texttt{kl\_stl}
from a fixed far, under-dispersed initialization
($m_0=m_\star+3\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}C_\star}$, $C_0=\tfrac12 C_\star$), with
mini-batches $B\in\{1,4,16\}$ and step $\Delta t=0.1$. The step is conservative:
near the optimum $C\approx C_\star=A^{-1}$, so the explicit mean map contracts as
$(1-\Delta t)$ \emph{independently of $\kappa$}, and both covariance updates are
unconditionally SPD for a log-concave target. Within a $(\text{config},B)$ cell
the baseline and STL methods share a common-random-number stream, so the
comparison is paired. We record the energy gap
$\mathcal{E}(a_n)-\mathcal{E}(a_\star)$ (analytic for the Gaussian target,
Gauss--Hermite for $\log\cosh$), the squared mean error, the relative covariance
Frobenius error, $W_2^2$ to $a_\star$, the covariance eigenvalue extremes, and an
SPD flag; the \emph{noise floor} is the energy gap averaged/medianed over the
final $20\%$ of iterations.

\paragraph{Grids and reproducibility.}
The Gaussian grid is $d\in\{2,8,32\}$, $\kappa\in\{10,100,10^4\}$; the $\log\cosh$
grid is $d\in\{2,8,32\}$, $\kappa\in\{10,100\}$, $\tau\in\{0.1,1\}$. Every cell
uses $20$ seeds. All computation is float64; the production grids run on a single
NVIDIA H200 (the algorithm grid optionally splits its cells across at most two
GPUs). Cell seeds are derived deterministically from the configuration, so a run
is reproducible and independent of the device split. Table~\ref{tab:stl-runtime}
records the environment and timing.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Estimator-level variance}

Figure~\ref{fig:stl-est} shows the Fisher--Rao variance ratio by state.
For the Gaussian target the ratio is numerically zero at the optimum and at every
mean-only perturbation (\emph{near}/\emph{medium}/\emph{far}), because those
states keep $C=C_\star$ and the STL fluctuation $(C^{-1}-A)(\theta-m)$ vanishes
identically. The only states with nonzero ratio are the dispersion states, and
they split the two regimes cleanly: \emph{over}-dispersion reduces the variance
(ratio $<1$) while \emph{under}-dispersion \emph{inflates} it (ratio $\approx 9$
at $\tfrac14 C_\star$), since $C^{-1}$ is then large and amplifies the residual.
For the misspecified $\log\cosh$ target the ratio is small but \emph{nonzero}
even at the optimum -- the nonlinear $\tanh$ part of the score is not removed by
the Gaussian residual -- and grows with the mean offset
(Figure~\ref{fig:stl-dist}). Table~\ref{tab:stl-est} reports the medians; the
Gaussian closed forms match the Monte Carlo estimates to within sampling error.

\subsection{Algorithm-level noise floor}

Figure~\ref{fig:stl-gap} shows the energy gap versus iteration for a
representative cell of each target. All four methods descend together during the
transient; once near the optimum the baseline methods flatten at a stochastic
noise floor, while on the Gaussian target the STL curves (dashed) keep
descending to machine precision. The reason is structural: the Gaussian Hessian
is state-independent, so the sampled covariance update is in fact deterministic
and converges exactly to $C_\star$; once $C=C_\star$ the STL mean estimator is
pointwise zero, so the mean stops fluctuating and the iterate lands on $a_\star$.
On the $\log\cosh$ target the Hessian sampler is genuinely stochastic and the
score residual cannot cancel the $\tanh$ term, so STL lowers the floor by about
three orders of magnitude but a residual floor remains. Figure~\ref{fig:stl-floor}
aggregates every cell: the Gaussian STL points sit on the display floor, and the
$\log\cosh$ points lie consistently below the diagonal. Table~\ref{tab:stl-floor}
gives the median reduction factors by scheme and batch size (the Gaussian STL
floor is reported as $<\!10^{-12}$), and Figure~\ref{fig:stl-batch} shows that
increasing the mini-batch lowers the baseline (and the $\log\cosh$ STL) floor
$\propto 1/B$ while preserving the STL advantage.

\section{Practical conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Mean-block STL is \emph{essentially cost-free} for Gaussian variational families,
because the Gaussian score $-C^{-1}(\theta-m)$ is available in closed form and is
already computed (via $C^{-1}$) by the KL covariance step. Within the regime it
was designed for -- a well-specified Gaussian target near the optimum -- it is
extremely effective: the mean-gradient variance is exactly zero whenever
$C=C_\star$, and the algorithm's stochastic noise floor drops by one to two orders
of magnitude. For a misspecified but smooth target it still helps near the
optimum, but it cannot remove the floor, because the residual estimator only
cancels the Gaussian part of the score. STL is therefore best presented as a
\emph{practical variance-reduction device}, not a theorem-level replacement for a
stochastic-variance assumption: it does not make the stochastic gradient vanish in
general, and under covariance under-dispersion it can even \emph{increase} the
variance.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item We test the \emph{direct} Hessian sampler, for which the covariance-block
residual rewrite is algebraically identical to the original update
(\S\ref{sec:theory}); the experiment therefore isolates mean-STL and says nothing
about Hessian-free \emph{pathwise} covariance gradients, where an STL-type
covariance correction \emph{is} a genuinely different estimator.
\item High-dimensional behavior is established empirically over $d\le 32$ and
diagonal curvature; the diagonal structure keeps the iterates diagonal, so the
$d$-dependence reported here is a controlled best case, not a worst-case bound.
\item The variance-inflation regime is reached through covariance
under-dispersion; the magnitude of the benefit (and of the inflation) depends on
how far $C$ is from $C_\star$ and on the degree of target misspecification, both
of which are problem-specific.
\end{itemize}
